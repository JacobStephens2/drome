# 75 Hard — Program Description

## What it is

**75 Hard** is a 75-day self-improvement program created by **Andy Frisella**, a St. Louis-based entrepreneur — co-founder and CEO of the supplement company 1st Phorm and of Supplement Superstores — and host of the *Real AF* podcast (previously *The MFCEO Project*). He launched the program in March 2019 on *The MFCEO Project* episode 290, "75HARD: A 75-Day Tactical Guide to Winning the War With Yourself" (published March 7, 2019), and re-presented it as episode 14 of the rebranded *Real AF* podcast on March 16, 2020. Frisella cites as inspiration James Lawrence — the "Iron Cowboy," who completed 50 Ironman-distance triathlons in 50 consecutive days across 50 US states — and Lawrence's philosophy that deliberately placing yourself in uncomfortable situations is what builds real mental toughness, along with Frisella's own two decades of experimenting on himself.

Frisella is emphatic that 75 Hard is **not a fitness challenge**. His official page states, in capitals, "THIS IS NOT A FITNESS CHALLENGE," and bills it as a "transformative mental toughness program" — in his words, an "Ironman for your brain." The claimed payoff is mental, not physical: confidence, self-belief, discipline, grit, fortitude, endurance, and perseverance, which he frames as "not traits you are born with, but skills you develop." Physical changes are described as "a fraction of the results," and he tells people whose only goal is getting in shape to do something else. The program itself is free; the rules are published on andyfrisella.com. (Frisella later monetized the surrounding content with a self-published book, *75 Hard: A Tactical Guide to Winning the War with Yourself*, released July 2, 2020, whose material was later folded into his expanded *The Book on Mental Toughness*.)

## The rules

Five tasks, **every day, for 75 consecutive days**:

1. **Follow a diet.** Any structured diet of your choosing, "geared toward your goals" (keto, paleo, vegetarian, etc.) — with **zero cheat meals and zero alcohol** for the full 75 days.
2. **Two 45-minute workouts, one of them outdoors** — regardless of weather. Per Frisella's official FAQ, the workouts cannot be stacked: each must be 45 *continuous* minutes (no splitting), with a minimum of 3–4 hours between the two sessions. The type of exercise is up to you — "it could literally be as simple as going for two 45-minute walks."
3. **Drink one gallon of water** (~3.8 L). Plain water only — per the FAQ, no mixers, additives, flavoring, or carbonation. Other beverages and the water content of food don't count toward the gallon, and can't offset it.
4. **Read 10 pages of a nonfiction book.** The FAQ specifies self-development or entrepreneurship/business books, read physically — "audiobooks do not count" (e-readers are generally accepted in secondary coverage and the community). You finish one book before starting another.
5. **Take a daily progress photo.**

### The enforcement rule

The program demands "zero compromises and zero substitutions." **Miss any task, on any day, for any reason, and you start over at Day 1** — "It doesn't matter if you're on Day 2 or Day 74." Frisella's illustration of the standard: "If you eat even a single chocolate chip... you fail." The official FAQ forecloses exceptions for travel, events, or one-off days. Even the weather rule isn't a carve-out: if conditions genuinely threaten your safety you should "use common sense and stay home," but the missed outdoor workout still resets you to Day 1. Frisella treats the urge to modify the rules as precisely the weakness the program targets: "That's the entire problem... you are trying to compromise and adapt things to suit your needs."

## The larger "Live Hard" program

75 Hard is the first stage — the "mental boot camp" — of Frisella's year-long **LIVEHARD** program, which has four stages meant to be completed within 12 months and repeated annually:

- **75 Hard** (75 days) — as above.
- **Phase 1** (30 days) — all five 75 Hard tasks, plus a daily 5-minute cold shower, a "power list" of three critical tasks, and 10 minutes of visualization. A mandatory 30-day break follows.
- **Phase 2** (30 days) — the core five tasks again, to "teach you the power of momentum."
- **Phase 3** (30 days, timed to end at the one-year anniversary) — everything from Phase 1, plus a daily substantive conversation with a stranger and a daily random act of kindness.

## Popularity and spin-offs

75 Hard became one of the biggest fitness-adjacent social-media phenomena of the 2020s. Launched in 2019, it found its mainstream breakthrough during the COVID-19 pandemic — ABC News traced the TikTok surge to a 22-year-old named Rylee Ollearis documenting her attempt from May to July 2020. The #75hard hashtag has accumulated well over a billion TikTok views (snapshots between roughly 1.1 and 1.3+ billion, depending on date), fed structurally by the format itself: participants post daily progress videos, so one person can generate 75+ posts — more if they restart. Frisella's site claims more than a million completions worldwide and 1.7 million+ Instagram posts tagged #75HARD (self-reported figures). An active Reddit community (r/75HARD) shares rules clarifications, milestone updates, and accountability threads, and Frisella's company ships an official 75 HARD tracking app (well-rated on iOS; it moved from a one-time purchase to a subscription, to considerable user complaint).

Its severity spawned gentler spin-offs:

- **75 Soft**: one 45-minute workout daily with one active-recovery day per week, eat well with alcohol limited to social occasions, 3 liters of water daily, 10 pages of any book — and no restart rule. (Attribution varies by outlet: Today and WebMD credit Irish TikTok fitness influencer Stephen Gallagher; Prevention and several dietitian reviews credit fitness coach Michael Cazayoux.)
- **75 Medium**: a TikTok-grown middle ground with no canonical rule set; common variants keep one daily 45-minute workout and add flexibility (e.g., a 90/10 diet, short daily meditation).

## Reception and criticism

Expert criticism has been extensive and consistent. Recurring points:

- **Not designed by health professionals.** Frisella is an entrepreneur and podcaster with no credentials in medicine, nutrition, or exercise science; success stories are anecdotal, and Cleveland Clinic sport psychologist Matthew Sacco, PhD, notes there is "little to no scientific evidence" the program is beneficial.
- **The all-or-nothing restart rule.** CNN fitness contributor Dana Santas argues (paraphrased in CNN's coverage) that forcing a restart after one slip can reinforce a cycle of perceived failure rather than building durable behavior change.
- **Disordered-eating risk.** Bethany Doerfler, senior clinical research dietitian at Northwestern Medicine, warns the rigid approach "can contribute to binge eating, disordered eating patterns, negative body image and negative self-talk." Independent dietitian reviews call the program "full of diet culture red flags" and effectively a diet despite its mental-toughness branding — particularly hazardous for anyone with an eating-disorder history.
- **Exercise volume.** A minimum of 630 workout minutes per week with no rest days far exceeds the HHS guideline of 150–300 minutes of moderate activity weekly; Cleveland Clinic and Penn State experts flag overtraining syndrome (persistent fatigue, declining performance, mood disturbance, suppressed immunity) and overuse-injury risk, especially for beginners. Penn State College of Medicine clinical exercise physiologist Brei Hummer-Bair: "it's really setting most people up for failure" — most people can't sustain even one daily 45-minute session.
- **The water rule.** A gallon (16 cups) exceeds typical recommendations (~9 cups of fluids for women, ~12.5 for men); needs vary by body size, activity, and climate, and heavy water intake combined with strict dieting and doubled exercise carries an electrolyte-imbalance (hyponatremia) risk. Emergency physician Robert Glatter, MD, told ABC News: "I think the routine puts a lot of strain on your body, primarily your kidneys. The risks include muscle breakdown."
- **Daily progress photos** can aggravate body-image and self-esteem problems.

Defenders and finishers report genuine value — discipline that carries into work and finances, confidence, time management, resilience, reduced decision fatigue from a fixed routine, and in some cases lasting sobriety — and even critical outlets concede the underlying habits (daily movement, hydration, reading, cutting alcohol, structure, accountability) are healthy ones. Trainers quoted in fitness media allow that it can jump-start a motivated person who thrives on hard external rules, while maintaining it is unsustainable for most. Frisella's own page carries a medical disclaimer, stated twice, telling users to consult a physician before starting.

## Who it tends to suit — and who experts say should avoid it

Coverage converges on a consistent profile. It potentially suits people with an existing consistent exercise base who want a rigid accountability framework, who thrive under binary rule systems, and who want to build habits across fitness, hydration, and reading simultaneously. Experts advise avoiding it (or proceeding only with medical guidance) for: beginners with no established fitness routine (the two-a-day requirement carries high injury and burnout risk), anyone with a history of eating disorders or disordered eating, people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, those with chronic health conditions, people prone to obsessive or all-or-nothing thinking — and, per the program's own disclaimer, anyone who hasn't consulted a physician first.
